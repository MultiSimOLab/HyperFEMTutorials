Tutorial 12: Pulsatile blood flow in a patient aorta

Source · Notebook · nbviewer

Blood is pumped by the heart into the aorta in short pulses and leaves it through the descending aorta and the three supra-aortic branches. We compute one cardiac cycle of the incompressible Navier–Stokes flow in a patient-specific (rigid) aorta, with the formulation of tutorial 11, a pulsatile inflow and three-element Windkessel models at the four outlets: each outlet sees the resistance and the compliance of the arterial tree downstream, which gives physiological pressures. The 3D flow and the four 0D models are coupled at every time step.

Measured on a Mac (M5 Max, 4 Julia threads, 4 BLAS threads, Julia 1.13.0, HyperFEM 0.3.0, HyperFEMKernels 0.2.6, Gridap 0.20.8): 16 659 free dofs (P1–P1 on 33 643 tetrahedra). FAST (10 time steps) 92 s and full (one cycle, 200 time steps) 170 s, including compilation (the first step takes about 24 s); 0.21 s per time step after that. One assembly of the step Jacobian (the check at the end): 0.06 s with the compiled backend, 0.65 s with Gridap.

What you will learn

  • boundary terms and lumped (0D) models in a TransientModel: Robin outlet conditions and backflow stabilisation in the weak form, and an explicit 3D–0D coupling through the presolver and internal hooks;
  • a time-dependent Dirichlet condition, BoundaryCondition(tag, profile, t -> Q(t));
  • the compiled backend of HyperFEMKernels (backend=:compiled) for a 3D stabilised flow problem, and how to check that it takes its fast paths (HyperFEMKernels.work_summary);
  • mass-conservation checks of a 3D–0D model and a comparison with a pure 0D model.

Problem statement

Find the velocity $\boldsymbol u$ and the pressure $p$ in the lumen $\Omega$ such that

\[\rho\left(\partial_t\boldsymbol u + (\nabla\boldsymbol u)\,\boldsymbol u\right) - \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \boldsymbol 0 , \qquad \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol u = 0 , \qquad \boldsymbol\sigma = -p\boldsymbol I + 2\mu\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\boldsymbol u) ,\]

with blood as a Newtonian fluid, $\rho = 1050$ kg/m³ and $\mu = 3.5$ mPa s. We work in mm, s and g: the density is $1.05\cdot 10^{-3}$ g/mm³, the viscosity $3.5\cdot 10^{-3}$ g/(mm s) = Pa s, and pressures come out in Pa.

Inlet. The flow rate is a half sine during systole (period $T = 1$ s, systole $T_s = 0.34$ s, stroke volume $V_s = 83.3$ ml) and zero in diastole:

\[Q(t) = \frac{\pi V_s}{2T_s}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T_s} \quad (0 \le t \le T_s), \qquad Q(t) = 0 \quad (T_s < t < T) .\]

The velocity at the inlet is $\boldsymbol u = Q(t)\,\boldsymbol g(\boldsymbol x)$, with $\boldsymbol g$ a plug profile along the mean inward normal that vanishes on the rim of the inlet (no slip) and carries a unit flow rate. Wall: no slip, $\boldsymbol u = \boldsymbol 0$ (rigid wall).

Outlets. At each outlet $\Gamma_i$ (descending aorta, brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid and left subclavian arteries) a three-element Windkessel (RCR) model relates the flow rate $Q_i = \int_{\Gamma_i}\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol n\,\mathrm d\Gamma$ and the pressure: a proximal resistance $Z_i$ in series with a capacitor $C_i$ (the compliance of the arteries downstream) that discharges through a distal resistance $R_i$,

\[\boldsymbol\sigma\boldsymbol n = -\left(P_{c,i} + Z_i Q_i\right)\boldsymbol n , \qquad C_i \frac{\mathrm dP_{c,i}}{\mathrm dt} = Q_i - \frac{P_{c,i}}{R_i} .\]

The compliance pressure $P_{c,i}$ is advanced with backward Euler using the flow rate of the previous step (explicit coupling), and the proximal term enters the weak form implicitly as a Robin term, $\int_{\Gamma_i} Z_i A_i\,(\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol n)(\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol n)\,\mathrm d\Gamma$, which equals $Z_i Q_i\int_{\Gamma_i}\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol n\,\mathrm d\Gamma$ when the normal velocity is uniform on the outlet (area $A_i$). The values of $Z_i$, $C_i$, $R_i$ are those of the aorta example of HyperFEMKernels.jl (examples/aorta_cfd/aorta_flow.jl): the descending aorta from Mousavi et al. (2021, Table 6, case «Aneurysmal 1») and the three supra-aortic branches B1–B3 from Youssefi et al. (2018, Table 1, subject A).

Backflow. During deceleration and diastole blood flows back into the domain through parts of the outlets, and the kinetic energy that enters there can make the computation blow up. The backflow stabilisation $-\rho\int_{\Gamma_i}(\boldsymbol w\cdot\boldsymbol n)_-\,\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol v\,\mathrm d\Gamma$, with $(a)_- = \min(a, 0)$, acts only where the flow enters (Bazilevs et al. 2009, DOI 10.1016/j.cma.2009.04.015; Esmaily-Moghadam et al. 2011, DOI 10.1007/s00466-011-0599-0).

Discretisation. As in tutorial 11: linear velocity and pressure (P1–P1) with SUPG, PSPG and LSIC stabilisation, BDF2 in time with an extrapolated advecting velocity $\boldsymbol w = 2\boldsymbol u^n - \boldsymbol u^{n-1}$ (an Oseen problem: one linear system per step), $\Delta t = 5$ ms. The element size is the edge of the regular tetrahedron with the volume of the cell, $h = (6\sqrt2\,|K|)^{1/3}$.

The constants read by functions evaluated at quadrature points (the stabilisation parameters) or by the inflow $Q(t)$ are const: a non-constant global read inside such a function is looked up dynamically at every evaluation, which makes the compiled Jacobian several times slower.

const ρ = 1050e-6                                              # g/mm³
const μ = 3.5e-3                                               # Pa s
const Tc = 1.0                                                 # s: cardiac period
const Ts = 0.34                                                # s: systole
const Vs = 83.3e3                                              # mm³: stroke volume (83.3 ml)
const Qmax = π * Vs / (2Ts)                                    # mm³/s: peak flow rate
Qin(t) = (τ = mod(t, Tc); τ <= Ts ? Qmax * sin(π * τ / Ts) : 0.0)
const mmHg = 133.322                                           # Pa

Geometry and mesh

A patient aorta (ascending aorta, arch with three branches and descending aorta), 33 643 linear tetrahedra, with the physical surfaces inlet, wall, outlet (descending aorta), B1, B2, B3 (branches). The mesh is coarse: most of its nodes lie on the wall, so the boundary layers are not resolved; it is meant to show the method, not to give converged wall shear stresses.

model = GmshDiscreteModel(joinpath(@__DIR__, "..", "data", "models", "aorta_fluido_h1p5_3p0.msh"))

GridapGmsh gives each physical surface its triangles, but an edge or a vertex shared by two surfaces belongs to only one of them. A Dirichlet condition needs all the nodes of its faces, so we add the edges and vertices of the faces of inlet and then of wall to their tags; the shared ones (the rim of the inlet) end up in the last tag, wall: the inflow profile vanishes on the rim.

function close_boundary_tags!(model, names)
  labels = get_face_labeling(model)
  topo = Gridap.Geometry.get_grid_topology(model)
  D = num_cell_dims(model)
  for name in names
    tag = findfirst(==(name), labels.tag_to_name)
    faces = findall(Gridap.Geometry.get_face_mask(labels, name, D - 1))
    for d in 0:D-2
      f_to_d = Gridap.Geometry.get_faces(topo, D - 1, d)
      ents = labels.d_to_dface_to_entity[d+1]
      newent = maximum(maximum(e) for e in labels.d_to_dface_to_entity) + 1
      for f in faces, e in f_to_d[f]
        ents[e] = newent
      end
      push!(labels.tag_to_entities[tag], newent)
    end
  end
  model
end
close_boundary_tags!(model, ["inlet", "wall"])

Ω = Triangulation(model)
dΩ = Measure(Ω, 3)
he = CellField(map(v -> (6sqrt(2) * v)^(1 / 3), collect(get_cell_measure(Ω))), Ω)   # element size
Γin = BoundaryTriangulation(model, tags="inlet")

n̄ = sum(∫(nin)dΓin) / Ain                                      # mean outward normal of the inlet

Windkessel parameters

The values are given in SI units (Pa s/m³ and m³/Pa) and converted to mm–s–g. ZA is $Z_i A_i$, the coefficient of the Robin term.

outs = ["outlet", "B1", "B2", "B3"]
SIR, SIC = 1e-9, 1e9                                           # Pa s/m³ → Pa s/mm³, m³/Pa → mm³/Pa
Z = [1.5e7, 1.36e8, 2.46e8, 1.74e8] .* SIR
C = [1.05e-8, 4.83e-10, 2.92e-10, 3.93e-10] .* SIC
R = [2.5e8, 9.23e8, 1.53e9, 1.13e9] .* SIR
Γo = [BoundaryTriangulation(model, tags=o) for o in outs]
dΓo = [Measure(Γ, 2) for Γ in Γo]
no = [get_normal_vector(Γ) for Γ in Γo]
ZA = [Z[i] * sum(∫(1.0)dΓo[i]) for i in eachindex(outs)]
flux(uh, i) = sum(∫(uh ⋅ no[i])dΓo[i])                         # outflow rate through outlet i

The same four Windkessel models attached to a rigid, inviscid aorta (a single node with pressure $P$) give a 0D model of the whole circulation downstream: $Q(t) = \sum_i (P - P_{c,i})/Z_i$ and $C_i\,\dot P_{c,i} = (P - P_{c,i})/Z_i - P_{c,i}/R_i$. Integrated over 30 cycles (RK4) it reaches its periodic state, which gives the compliance pressures at $t = 0$ (the initial values of the 3D computation: the flow starts at rest, at the end of diastole) and a reference for the pressures and the split of the flow.

function windkessel_0d(Qin, Z, C, R; T, h=1e-4, ncycles=30)
  P0(t, Pc) = (Qin(t) + sum(Pc ./ Z)) / sum(1 ./ Z)
  f(t, Pc) = ((P0(t, Pc) .- Pc) ./ Z .- Pc ./ R) ./ C
  rk4(t, y) = (k1 = f(t, y); k2 = f(t + h / 2, y + h / 2 * k1); k3 = f(t + h / 2, y + h / 2 * k2);
               k4 = f(t + h, y + h * k3); y + h / 6 * (k1 + 2k2 + 2k3 + k4))
  nper = round(Int, T / h)
  Qm = sum(Qin(k * h) for k in 0:nper-1) / nper
  Pc = R ./ (Z .+ R) .* (Qm / sum(1 ./ (Z .+ R)))             # mean state as the starting point
  for k in 0:ncycles*nper-1
    Pc = rk4(k * h, Pc)
  end
  Pc0 = copy(Pc)
  ts, P, Q = Float64[], Float64[], Vector{Float64}[]           # one period of the periodic state
  for k in 0:nper
    t = k * h; p = P0(t, Pc)
    push!(ts, t); push!(P, p); push!(Q, (p .- Pc) ./ Z)
    Pc = rk4(t, Pc)
  end
  (; Pc0, ts, P, Q)
end
ref0d = windkessel_0d(Qin, Z, C, R; T=Tc)
@printf("0D model: P from %.1f to %.1f mmHg, Pc(0) = %s mmHg\n", minimum(ref0d.P) / mmHg, maximum(ref0d.P) / mmHg,
        string(round.(ref0d.Pc0 ./ mmHg, digits=1)))

Boundary conditions and finite element spaces

The plug profile is the interpolation of $-\bar{\boldsymbol n}$ on the inlet with zero on the wall (and so on the rim); its discrete flow rate $q_1$ normalises it, so that the imposed flow rate is exactly $Q(t)$. The profile and the time function are given separately: BoundaryCondition("inlet", g, Qin) imposes $\boldsymbol g(\boldsymbol x)\,Q(t)$. The profile is built by a function that returns a closure, so that the pointwise function reads its own captured value and not a global variable.

reffeu = ReferenceFE(lagrangian, VectorValue{3,Float64}, 1)
reffep = ReferenceFE(lagrangian, Float64, 1)
uniform(a) = x -> a
Vaux = TestFESpace(model, reffeu; conformity=:H1, dirichlet_tags=["inlet", "wall"])
u1 = FEFunction(TrialFESpace(Vaux, [uniform(-n̄), uniform(zero(n̄))]), zeros(num_free_dofs(Vaux)))
q1 = -sum(∫(u1 ⋅ nin)dΓin)
D_bc = MultiFieldBC([
  BoundaryConditions(
    BoundaryCondition("inlet", uniform(-n̄ / q1), Qin),
    BoundaryCondition("wall", [0.0, 0.0, 0.0])),
  NothingBC()])
Vu = TestFESpace(model, reffeu, D_bc[1], conformity=:H1)
Vp = TestFESpace(model, reffep, D_bc[2], conformity=:H1)
V = MultiFieldFESpace([Vu, Vp])
U = MultiFieldFESpace([TrialFESpace(Vu, D_bc[1], 0.0), TrialFESpace(Vp, D_bc[2], 0.0)])

Weak form

The volume terms are those of tutorial 11 (with $h$ of tetrahedra); the outlets add the Robin term of the proximal resistances and the backflow stabilisation, which are part of the operator, and the compliance pressures $P_{c,i}$, which are known numbers of the step and only enter the residual. Pc holds the compliance pressures of the step being computed and Pcn those of the last accepted step.

Pcn = copy(ref0d.Pc0)
Pc = copy(Pcn)
τsupg(u, h) = ((2 * norm(u) / h)^2 + 9 * (4μ / (ρ * h^2))^2)^(-1 / 2)
τpspg(u, h) = τsupg(u, h) / ρ
τlsic(u, h) = ρ * norm(u) * h * 0.5
ε(∇u) = 0.5 * (∇u + ∇u')
negpart(a) = 0.5 * (a - abs(a))
function advecting(cu)
  a, b = cu.step == 1 ? (1.0, 0.0) : (2.0, 1.0)
  a * cu.u⁻ - b * cu.u⁻⁻
end

The bilinear part of a step, with the advecting velocity w, the velocity un of the previous step (for the $\tau$'s) and the time derivative ut of the unknown:

function step_form(w, un, (u, p), ut, (v, q))
  τs = τsupg ∘ (un, he); τp = τpspg ∘ (un, he); τl = τlsic ∘ (un, he)
  Ru = ρ * ut + ρ * (∇(u)' ⋅ w)
  a = ∫(ρ * (ut ⋅ v))dΩ + ∫(2μ * (ε ∘ (∇(u))) ⊙ (ε ∘ (∇(v))))dΩ + ∫(ρ * ((∇(u)' ⋅ w) ⋅ v))dΩ -
      ∫(p * (∇ ⋅ v))dΩ - ∫(q * (∇ ⋅ u))dΩ +
      ∫(τs * ((∇(v)' ⋅ w) ⋅ Ru))dΩ + ∫(τs * ((∇(v)' ⋅ w) ⋅ ∇(p)))dΩ -
      ∫(τp * (∇(q) ⋅ Ru))dΩ - ∫(τp * (∇(q) ⋅ ∇(p)))dΩ +
      ∫(τl * (∇ ⋅ v) * (∇ ⋅ u))dΩ
  for i in eachindex(outs)
    a = a + ∫(ZA[i] * (u ⋅ no[i]) * (v ⋅ no[i]))dΓo[i] - ∫(ρ * (negpart ∘ (w ⋅ no[i])) * (u ⋅ v))dΓo[i]
  end
  a
end
function res(ctx)
  cu = ctx[1]
  un = cu.u⁻; w = advecting(cu)
  ((u, p), (v, q)) -> begin
    r = step_form(w, un, (u, p), ∂t(cu, u), (v, q))
    for i in eachindex(outs)
      r = r + ∫(Pc[i] * (v ⋅ no[i]))dΓo[i]
    end
    r
  end
end
function jac(ctx)
  cu = ctx[1]
  un = cu.u⁻; w = advecting(cu)
  ((u, p), (du, dp), (v, q)) -> step_form(w, un, (du, dp), δ∂t(cu, du), (v, q))
end

Solver and computational model

backend=:compiled assembles with the compiled, threaded kernels of HyperFEMKernels. The operator is compiled in the first step; in the next steps only the numbers that change (the BDF coefficients, the compliance pressures, the Dirichlet values) are read again from the weak form, and the compiled code, the sparsity pattern and the tables are reused. The step system has 16 659 unknowns: a sparse LU factorisation costs about 0.06 s, as much as one assembly of the Jacobian, so a direct solver is the right choice at this size (tutorial 13 shows the iterative solvers for larger problems).

The 0D models are advanced with two hooks. presolver(t, Δt) runs before every attempt of a step: it computes the compliance pressures of the attempt from the flow rates of the last accepted state. internal(xh, xh⁻) runs when the step is accepted: it makes them the new accepted values. With a fixed time step there are no rejected attempts, but the pair Pc/Pcn keeps the coupling right if a step were halved.

nls = NewtonRaphson(LUSolver(); verbose=0)
accept_windkessel(xh, xh⁻) = (Pcn .= Pc)
flow = TransientModel(res, jac, U, V, D_bc; scheme=BDF(2), tangent_update=:step, nls=nls,
                      backend=:compiled, internal=accept_windkessel)

The flow rates of the last accepted state are computed once, by the post-processing function below (which runs after every accepted step), and kept in Qn (zero at rest, before the first step).

Qn = zeros(length(outs))
function windkessel!(t, Δt)
  for i in eachindex(outs)
    Pc[i] = (Pcn[i] + Δt * Qn[i] / C[i]) / (1 + Δt / (R[i] * C[i]))
  end
end

Post-processing

After every step we store the inflow rate (measured on the solution), the four outflow rates, the compliance pressures, the outlet pressures $P_{c,i} + Z_iQ_i$, the mean pressure on the inlet and the largest velocity component; the flow is written to VTK a few times per cycle.

Δt = 0.005
nsteps = FAST ? 10 : round(Int, Tc / Δt)
vtk_every = FAST ? nsteps : 20
hist = (t=Float64[], qin=Float64[], qout=Vector{Float64}[], Pc=Vector{Float64}[], Pout=Vector{Float64}[],
        pin=Float64[], umax=Float64[], wall=Float64[])
tclock = Ref(time())
function record(post)
  uh, ph = get_state(flow)
  t = post.Λ[end]
  q = [flux(uh, i) for i in eachindex(outs)]
  Qn .= q
  push!(hist.t, t); push!(hist.qin, -sum(∫(uh ⋅ nin)dΓin)); push!(hist.qout, q); push!(hist.Pc, copy(Pc))
  push!(hist.Pout, Pc .+ Z .* q); push!(hist.pin, sum(∫(ph)dΓin) / Ain)
  push!(hist.umax, maximum(abs, get_free_dof_values(uh))); push!(hist.wall, time() - tclock[])
  tclock[] = time()
  if FAST || post.iter % 10 == 0
    @printf("step %3d  t = %.3f s  Q_in = %6.1f ml/s  Q_out = %s ml/s  P_in = %5.1f mmHg  %.2f s\n", post.iter, t,
            hist.qin[end] / 1e3, string(round.(q ./ 1e3, digits=1)), hist.pin[end] / mmHg, hist.wall[end])
  end
  if post.iter % vtk_every == 0
    get_pvd(post)[t] = createvtk(Ω, joinpath(outdir, "aorta_$(post.iter)"), cellfields=["u" => uh, "p" => ph])
  end
end
post = PostProcessor(flow, record; is_vtk=true, filepath=outdir)

Checks

Mass conservation. The pressure test functions include the constants, and with $q = 1$ the weak form says $\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol u\,\mathrm d\Omega = 0$ (the stabilisation terms contain $\nabla q$): the inflow equals the sum of the outflows at every step, to the precision of the linear solver. The measured inflow is the imposed $Q(t)$. The tolerance of these checks and of the volume budget below is $10^{-10}$ relative to the peak flow: the LU solution is accurate to about $10^{-12}$ (we measured $9\cdot 10^{-13}$ for the mass balance), and the budget inherits that error.

qout_sum = [sum(q) for q in hist.qout]
mass_err = maximum(abs.(hist.qin .- qout_sum)) / Qmax
inflow_err = maximum(abs.(hist.qin .- Qin.(hist.t))) / Qmax
println("max |Q_in - ΣQ_out| / Q_max = $mass_err,  max |Q_in - Q(t)| / Q_max = $inflow_err")
@assert flag == 1 "the time loop did not complete"
@assert length(hist.t) == nsteps "a time step was bisected"
@assert mass_err < 1e-10 "mass is not conserved: max |Q_in - ΣQ_out| / Q_max = $mass_err"
@assert inflow_err < 1e-10 "the inflow rate differs from Q(t): $inflow_err"

Volume budget of the 3D–0D system. The volume that entered up to the previous step is stored in the compliances or has left through the distal resistances: summing the update of $P_{c,i}$ over the steps, $\Delta t\sum_{k<N}Q_{in}^k = \sum_i C_i\,(P_{c,i}^N - P_{c,i}^0) + \Delta t\sum_{k\le N}\sum_i P_{c,i}^k/R_i$ (the flow rate is lagged by one step, and $Q^0 = 0$: the flow starts at rest).

Vin = Δt * sum(hist.qin[1:end-1])
Vstored = sum(C .* (hist.Pc[end] .- ref0d.Pc0))
Vdrained = Δt * sum(sum(Pk ./ R) for Pk in hist.Pc)
budget_err = abs(Vin - Vstored - Vdrained) / (Δt * nsteps * Qmax)
@printf("volume budget: in %.1f ml = stored %.1f ml + drained %.1f ml  (relative error %.1e)\n",
        Vin / 1e3, Vstored / 1e3, Vdrained / 1e3, budget_err)
@assert budget_err < 1e-10 "the volume budget of the Windkessel models does not close: $budget_err"

Physiological range. The pressures at the outlets and at the inlet stay in the physiological range of the systemic arteries, and the velocity stays bounded (the backflow stabilisation does its job).

Pall = reduce(vcat, hist.Pout)
@printf("outlet pressures %.1f–%.1f mmHg, inlet %.1f–%.1f mmHg, max velocity component %.0f mm/s\n",
        minimum(Pall) / mmHg, maximum(Pall) / mmHg, minimum(hist.pin) / mmHg, maximum(hist.pin) / mmHg, maximum(hist.umax))

  @assert isapprox(hist.pin[end], 10492.936373140392, rtol=1e-5) "regression: P_in = $(hist.pin[end])"
end

Comparison with the 0D model (full run)

Over one cycle the 3D–0D model and the 0D model see the same inflow and the same Windkessel models; they differ by the resistance and the inertia of the aorta itself and by the spatial distribution of the pressure at the outlets. We compare the volume that leaves through each outlet in the cycle and the extreme pressures of the descending aorta. We measured differences of 0.2–2.7 % in the volumes (the branches, whose flow depends more on the 3D pressure distribution, differ most) and of about 1 % in the extreme pressures; the tolerances are 5 % and 3 %.

if !FAST
  vol3d = Δt .* reduce(+, hist.qout)
  w0 = [k == 1 || k == length(ref0d.ts) ? 0.5 : 1.0 for k in eachindex(ref0d.ts)]
  vol0d = (ref0d.ts[2] - ref0d.ts[1]) .* reduce(+, w0 .* ref0d.Q)
  Pdesc = [P[1] for P in hist.Pout]
  println("outlet                volume 3D (ml)   volume 0D (ml)")
  for i in eachindex(outs)
    @printf("  %-8s           %10.2f       %10.2f\n", outs[i], vol3d[i] / 1e3, vol0d[i] / 1e3)
  end
  @printf("descending aorta: P 3D %.1f–%.1f mmHg, 0D %.1f–%.1f mmHg\n", minimum(Pdesc) / mmHg, maximum(Pdesc) / mmHg,
          minimum(ref0d.P) / mmHg, maximum(ref0d.P) / mmHg)
  @assert all(isapprox.(vol3d ./ sum(vol3d), vol0d ./ sum(vol0d), rtol=0.05)) "flow split away from the 0D model"
  @assert isapprox(maximum(Pdesc), maximum(ref0d.P), rtol=0.03) "systolic pressure away from the 0D model"
  @assert isapprox(minimum(Pdesc), minimum(ref0d.P), rtol=0.03) "diastolic pressure away from the 0D model"
end

Efficiency of the compiled backend (full run)

With Gridap's assembly (backend=:lazy) the Jacobian alone takes 0.65 s and dominates the step; with the compiled backend a step of 0.21 s splits into Jacobian 0.06 s, residual 0.04 s, LU 0.06 s and the rest (see below). Running this tutorial with backend=:lazy gives the same solution (‖x‖ equal to 1e-13 in FAST) at about 1.0 s per step. We build, on the final state, the Jacobian of a BDF2 step with the same forms, assemble it with Gridap and with a KernelOperator of HyperFEMKernels (what backend=:compiled does inside the model), and check that both give the same matrix and that the compiled one is at least four times faster: we measured eleven times, and a non-constant global read by a stabilisation parameter (see the setup) makes the compiled Jacobian about eight times slower, which this margin catches. HyperFEMKernels.work_summary lists the path each group of integrals takes: :factorised or :linear_maps are the fast paths; :duals (automatic differentiation at every point) would point at a weak form or a pointwise function the compiler could not analyse. We measured (4 threads) 0.06 s for the compiled Jacobian and 0.65 s for Gridap's, with the volume group and the four outlet groups all :factorised and nothing left to Gridap. Inside the model a step costs about 0.21 s: Jacobian 0.06 s, residual 0.04 s, LU factorisation 0.06 s, and the rest is the update of the operator with the numbers of the step and the post-processing.

if !FAST
  uN = get_state(flow)[1]
  c0 = 1.5 / Δt
  kres(x, y) = step_form(uN, uN, x, c0 * x[1], y)
  kjac(x, dx, y) = step_form(uN, uN, dx, c0 * dx[1], y)
  x0 = zeros(num_free_dofs(U))
  gop = Gridap.FESpaces.get_algebraic_operator(FEOperator(kres, kjac, U, V))
  kop = KernelOperator(kres, kjac, U, V)
  Ag = Gridap.Algebra.allocate_jacobian(gop, x0)
  Ak = Gridap.Algebra.allocate_jacobian(kop, x0)
  Gridap.Algebra.jacobian!(Ag, gop, x0); Gridap.Algebra.jacobian!(Ak, kop, x0)   # warm-up (compilation)
  tg = minimum(@elapsed(Gridap.Algebra.jacobian!(Ag, gop, x0)) for _ in 1:3)
  tk = minimum(@elapsed(Gridap.Algebra.jacobian!(Ak, kop, x0)) for _ in 1:3)
  ws = HyperFEMKernels.work_summary(kop)
  for g in ws.groups
    g.kind === :jacobian && println("  Jacobian group $(g.group): $(g.domain), $(g.cells) cells, path $(g.path)")
  end
  @printf("Jacobian assembly: compiled %.3f s, Gridap %.3f s (%d threads); fallback groups: %d\n", tk, tg,
          Threads.nthreads(), ws.fallback_jacobian)
  @assert norm(Ak - Ag) < 1e-10 * norm(Ag) "the compiled Jacobian differs from Gridap's"
  @assert ws.fallback_jacobian == 0 "part of the Jacobian falls back to Gridap"
  @assert all(g -> g.path !== :duals, filter(g -> g.kind === :jacobian, ws.groups)) "a Jacobian group takes the slow :duals path"
  @assert tk < tg / 4 "the compiled Jacobian ($tk s) is not four times faster than Gridap's ($tg s)"
end

Going further

  • Two or more cycles: nsteps = 2 * round(Int, Tc / Δt). The compliance pressures start from the periodic state of the 0D model, so the second cycle differs little from the first.
  • Taylor–Hood P2–P1 (99 141 dofs on this mesh) needs no PSPG and resolves the flow better; with quadratic velocities the SUPG residual also contains the viscous term $-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol u^n$, which must be computed (for instance by projecting $\nabla\boldsymbol u^n$ onto discontinuous P1 functions).
  • A realistic inflow profile, from the solution of a Poisson problem on the inlet plus an eccentric valve jet, is in examples/aorta_cfd/aorta_flow.jl of HyperFEMKernels.jl, which also compares the assembly of that P2–P1 problem with Gridap and with HyperFEMKernels.
  • backend=:lazy assembles with Gridap: same solution, slower steps.
